TRANSMISSION 004 CULTURE.EXE / READING CULTURE

PLAYLISTS FIRST,
STORIES NEXT.

The book, the experience, and everything being sold in between.

BY NAMITHA RAJEEV ARTICLE 004 / AVAILABLE

It began with a playlist.

‘Maula Mere’ was part of the official playlist for Ana Huang’s King of Gluttony. Its presence inside the book wasn’t accidental. It read less like a mood choice than a calculated one — a way of courting Indian readers specifically, rather than simply scoring a scene. That’s the thing about these playlists: they’re rarely just a bonus. An actual Spotify link, tucked near the acknowledgments, as if the novel wasn’t quite finished without a soundtrack. What looks like a gimmick at first turns out to be a pattern. Sarah J. Maas does it. Colleen Hoover does it. Half the romance shelf now comes with its own audio companion, and some authors have gone further. Taylor Jenkins Reid’s Daisy Jones & The Six produced an actual album for its fictional band, a soundtrack for a group that never existed.

The implication is hard to miss: readers no longer simply read books. They experience them, and the experience is what is being sold. Books used to be read. Now they are consumed, in the fuller sense of the word: listened to, eaten alongside, worn, displayed and visited.

“The story is the pitch. The experience is the product.”

For most of publishing history, a book’s job was simple — contain the words, put an attractive cover on it, send it into the world. The story was the pitch. Marketing stopped at the blurb and, occasionally, a review quote on the back. An author wrote the manuscript and then, largely, stepped away. Publicity was somebody else’s department, and reading was a solitary act: one person, one book, no audience.

None of that holds quite as true anymore, and the reason is the oldest one in commerce. A book is a single purchase. An experience can be bought again and again, and consumption runs on repeat.

Attention, not information, is now the limited resource, and a book competes with a feed rather than a bookshelf. A feed rewards what is visual, musical and shareable, which a static block of text cannot be on its own. So the object itself was redesigned. Sprayed edges, hidden art beneath the dust jacket, “BookTok edition” covers made for readers who found the book through a video rather than a shelf. Special editions are sold not as better copies, but as different experiences of the same story.

When the object isn’t enough, the world around it gets stocked. There are official cookbooks for Bridgerton and A Song of Ice and Fire, candles named after specific characters, even a perfume designed to smell like a freshly printed book. Taste and smell, once the things a story could only describe, are now things a story can sell you.

Social media compounds the effect by turning reading into something to display. A shelf, colour-coded tabs, a carefully cultivated “reading aesthetic” — all of it folds reading into a performed identity rather than a private habit. Since performance wants an audience, reading has become communal : read-a-thons, buddy reads, Discord book clubs, BookTok reactions filmed mid-sob. Reading, once private, is now something people do together, in real time, on camera.

Authors, meanwhile, have become their own marketing departments. Self-publishing and TikTok put promotion directly into writers’ hands, so elements that once felt like bolted-on extras, like playlists, mood boards and bonus scenes, are now built into how a book is made in the first place.

None of this is as new as it feels. Victorian readers waited on street corners for the next serialized chapter of Dickens. Illustrated editions, literary salons, fan letters — the impulse is old. Even place has long been part of the package: pilgrims have walked to Haworth and Jane Austen’s House for generations. What has changed is the scale. Now there are Harry Potter theme parks, hostels where guests sleep in bookshelf-shaped bunks, silent reading parties held in bars. And the experience is built into the book from the start, rather than added on after a story happens to succeed.

Readers have long described reading as “getting lost in a story” or “travelling the world from home.” Perhaps the playlists and limited-edition covers are simply noise. Or perhaps they are that same old instinct, rebranded for a generation that finds even a thirty-second reel too long.

“When the experience is the product, it never has to end.”

That is what consumption looks like here. When the story is the product, a reader’s appetite ends at the last page. When the experience is the product, it never has to.

The book, it turns out, was never just the book. The industry has simply stopped pretending otherwise.
