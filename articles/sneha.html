THE BORROWED
IMAGINATION.

Are we still imagining, or have we only gotten skilled at reproducing what we scroll past?

BY SNEHA A. CREATIVITY / DIGITAL CULTURE

RECOVERED TEXT / ARTICLE 007
Are we still imagining, or have we only gotten skilled at reproducing what we scroll past?

I wanted to create something original. So I opened Pinterest. I wanted to draw something beautiful, so I opened Instagram and searched until I found beauty someone else had already made. I wanted to rearrange my room, so I scrolled through other people's rooms, already arranged, already lit, already captioned. Before I let myself begin anything, I went looking for what the ending was supposed to look like, and the feed was always willing to show me. It felt harmless. Natural, even. Isn't that where creation starts, with a borrowed spark? But somewhere between the scrolling and the finding, a quiet question started following me around: was I still imagining anything, or just choosing from a grid someone else had already curated for me?

We live inside an ocean of other people's seeing, and most days we enter it through the same small rectangle in our hand. One scroll and a hundred lives arrive at once: other people's mornings, other people's rooms, other people's faces arranged into their best angle, all within the time it takes to breathe. We can pass through more curated beauty in an afternoon than someone born two centuries ago might have seen in an entire lifetime. We can watch strangers live in apartments we'll never afford, visit coastlines we'll never stand on, wear a kind of ease that took three takes to get right. Inspiration has never been closer. And still, I wonder whether having so much of it, arriving so constantly, has made it harder to find the part of anything that is still, somehow, only mine.

Imagination has always borrowed, this much is true, and always has been. No one creates in silence, untouched. A writer carries the quiet weight of every book that ever moved them. A painter's eye has been colored by skies that belonged to someone else first. What's different now is the speed and the sameness of the borrowing. A musician reshaping a sound that passed through centuries is one thing; a feed reshaping a million people's taste into the same six aesthetics by Tuesday is another. We are made from what has touched us. But Instagram doesn't touch us once and let go. It touches us a hundred times a day, in the same voice, until we start to wonder whether originality was ever about starting from nothing, or whether it was always this: taking what we were given and turning it, just slightly, toward something the algorithm hadn't already decided we'd like.

"We are made from what has touched us."

But there is a difference between being fed by something and quietly becoming dependent on it.

Think of the blank page. It used to feel like an open door, a small, nervous invitation into not-knowing. Now, more often, it sends me reaching for my phone instead: for a reel that already said it better, for someone else's finished caption, for the kind of opening line that already proved, in someone else's comment section, that it could work. The same pull runs through drawing, through photography, through the small daily decisions of life: what to wear, how to plate dinner, how to sit in a chair so a photo of it looks like something. We rarely step toward something unfamiliar anymore without first checking what the admired, algorithm-approved version looks like. We want to know, before we've even tried, what performs and without quite meaning to, we let the feed start giving orders.

The danger was never in looking. The danger is mistaking what is familiar, what keeps appearing, keeps getting liked, keeps getting saved, for what is possible. After a thousand sunsets shot from the same beach, in the same filter, captioned with some version of the same four words, it becomes harder to picture one that refuses the pattern. After enough photographs of "loneliness" — the same empty chair, the same rain against the same glass, the same moody desaturation — we start to believe the feeling is that chair, that glass, that filter, instead of whatever it actually is in us. We don't only inherit the images. We inherit the whole visual grammar the app has quietly trained into us. And an imagination that was built to wander keeps finding itself back on a path a thousand other accounts have already worn smooth with scrolling. Maybe this is why the fear of being unoriginal feels so close to anyone who tries to make something now. Every app is also, quietly, a leaderboard. Every idea seems to already have ten thousand likes somewhere. Every metaphor feels slightly used, every aesthetic already has a name, a hashtag, a following of its own. The more we scroll, the more we feel the crowd standing just behind us, all of them making, too, and posting it, and being told it's good. And the question quietly shifts from what do I want to say to has this already been said, posted, and said better?

That question alone can be enough to stop a person cold. We start editing a thought before it has finished arriving, the way we'd edit a photo before anyone's even seen it. We throw away a drawing for looking too much like something we scrolled past last week. We hesitate over a sentence because somewhere, someone with more followers has already written something close to it, and lovelier, and it got reposted. In trying so hard not to repeat what the feed has already shown us, we sometimes manage to make nothing at all, just close the app, and open it again. And there is something else quietly slipping away in all this scrolling: our ability to simply witness something without immediately turning it into a shape we already recognize from somewhere online.

We have photographed our own sunsets before truly looking at them, saved other people's travel photos before booking the trip, bookmarked the places we hope to someday stand inside, already knowing the exact angle we'll take the exact same picture from. We already know what a cinematic rainy afternoon is supposed to look like, what a café has to feel like to count as "aesthetic," what a peaceful morning is contractually meant to resemble, because we have seen it, posed and lit, a thousand times before breakfast. We've learned the visual language of experience before we've had the experience itself. A place can arrive on our screen a hundred times before our feet ever touch its ground, and by the time they do, we are sometimes only checking the world against the post, not the other way around: mentally cropping, already composing the caption, already half-elsewhere.

Which is maybe why boredom deserves a little more faith than we give it. We have become remarkably good at making sure we never feel it: waiting for a bus, standing in a line, sitting alone with a cup going cold, and instead of letting the moment sit there empty, we open the same three apps, in the same order, without deciding to. But it is often in those unguarded, undecorated moments, the ones we've trained ourselves to feel instantly that a thought actually wanders somewhere of its own accord, that two unrelated memories brush against each other and make a small spark neither one had alone. Nothing we scroll through was built to give us that. It was built to keep us scrolling. Maybe imagination simply needs a little unclaimed quiet, a stretch of time where nothing is asking for our thumb, and no feed has already decided what we're supposed to see, or feel, or want.

"The imagination doesn't need to be untouched to be ours."

None of this is an argument for deleting the apps, for turning away from everything we take in. That would be impossible, and it would be a loss, too. Art has always grown in the company of other art, and that's true even when the art arrives through a feed. Someone's photography on Instagram can genuinely retrain an eye for color. A reel can introduce a sound, a place, a way of moving through the world, that we'd never have found on our own. What we consume even there, even like that, can widen the imagination, because it carries us past the edge of what we already knew we wanted.

So maybe the real question was never how much we consume, but what we let it become once it's inside us. Do we let it unsettle what we assumed, bend how we see, fold itself into something larger and slower than imitation? Or do we only collect its surface — screenshot, saving to a folder we'll never open again, admiring, copying, never quite letting it change shape inside us, reproducing what moved us instead of discovering what we might do, now that we have been moved? Maybe the imagination doesn't need to be untouched to be ours. It only needs room to transform what it has already taken in, even what it took in through a feed, half-distracted, at two in the morning. We will always be borrowers now, of color, of voice, of rhythm, of a hundred strangers' curated mornings. But borrowing was never meant to mean surrendering the part of us that can still see past what the algorithm decided to show us.

I come back to the blank page. This time, I left my phone in the other room. It's uncomfortable — no reference to lean against, no finished example pointing the way forward, no small red notification promising that someone, somewhere, already approved of something like this. For a moment, nothing comes. Then a thought arrives, a little crooked, a little unfinished, unmistakably mine, and this time, I let it stay. Maybe that is where creativity begins again: not in deleting everything we've scrolled through, but in finally finding the nerve, for a little while, to put the phone down and walk past it.
