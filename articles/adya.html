"Sometimes the real challenge is not avoiding trends, but remembering that creativity does not always have to follow a recognisable pattern."

There is a certain familiarity to scrolling through Instagram Reels. A particular song begins playing, followed by a sequence of movements, transitions or expressions that you have probably encountered several times before. Different people, different locations, perhaps different outfits, but essentially the same idea. Yet, every creator hopes that their version will be the one that takes off.

I find myself doing this too. Whenever I make a dance reel, I wonder whether it will trend, even when I am using the same music and choreography as everyone else. Perhaps it is the background, the presentation or some little detail that makes one reel more successful than another.

This isn't limited to dance videos. Across social media, trends have begun influencing how we create, present and consume almost everything. There are familiar formats for reels, familiar aesthetics for photographs, familiar sounds for videos and familiar structures for stories. Eventually, even the supposedly spontaneous act of creating something starts looking predictable.

01 / THE FAMILIAR FORMAT

Take the song I See Red, for instance. It became a popular choice for rampwalk videos, often accompanying some particularly dramatic stunt or movement. After seeing it enough times, I began recognising what was coming even before the performance started. The surprise had disappeared. I knew what to expect, and so did everyone else.

That is one of the peculiar things about internet trends. They are exciting when they begin, but their popularity often leads to repetition. Something that was once unusual becomes a format, and the format becomes something everyone knows how to reproduce.

"Something that was once unusual becomes a format, and the format becomes something everyone knows how to reproduce."
02 / THE BOOKTOK EFFECT

As a writer, I have noticed a similar pattern in contemporary fiction. Certain tropes have become enormously popular through platforms such as BookTok and Bookstagram. These communities have helped books reach readers who might never have discovered them otherwise. They have also influenced what publishers choose to promote and what readers increasingly look for when selecting their next book.

There is nothing inherently wrong with enjoying a particular trope. Readers have always had preferences, and writers have always drawn inspiration from popular genres. But, have we become so used to familiar tropes that we approach stories with a checklist of expectations? We know what kind of protagonist we want, what sort of romantic tension we enjoy and, occasionally, even what the ending should look like.

03 / WRITING FOR THE ALGORITHM

I generally prefer softer romance and happily ever afters, but there is a part of me that wonders whether the kind of fiction I enjoy writing would find an audience today. Dark romance is popular, certain character archetypes are everywhere, and particular tropes seem to have become almost essential selling points. The temptation to write something that fits the current market is real, even when it isn't necessarily the story I want to tell.

"What happens when creators begin thinking about popularity before they think about the work itself?"

That brings us to an uncomfortable question: what happens when creators begin thinking about popularity before they think about the work itself?

04 / HOW THE ALGORITHM THINKS

Algorithms are designed to identify patterns in what people watch, read, like and share. Then it makes sense that content resembling something that’s already performed well can be an attractive choice for creators who want to reach a wider audience. If a format works, others follow it. If a style receives attention, it gets reproduced. There is nothing particularly surprising about people wanting their work to be seen. After all, creating something and sharing it with others is often part of the pleasure.

But when following familiar patterns becomes the safer option, experimentation can become discouraging. Sometimes creators deliberately avoid trying something different because they fear it will not perform well. Other times, they simply become accustomed to reproducing what works. Either way, the space for unfamiliar ideas begins to feel smaller.

05 / IS TRENDING REALLY THE PROBLEM?

Trends aren’t necessarily bad for creativity. They can introduce people to new art forms, encourage experimentation and give independent creators opportunities they might not otherwise have had. Social media has made it possible for artists, writers, musicians and performers to find audiences without depending entirely on traditional institutions. That is worth celebrating.

"Perhaps the real question is whether being visible is the same as being seen."

Yet, I wonder whether being visible is the same as being seen. A creator might receive thousands of views for following a popular format, but does that mean people have noticed their individual creativity? A writer might sell books because their story contains the tropes currently in demand, but does that mean readers are discovering something new? Visibility is valuable, certainly, but it should not become the only measure of creative success.

06 / THE SPACE TO BE DIFFERENT

Perhaps the real challenge is not to avoid trends altogether, but to remember that creativity does not always have to follow a recognisable pattern. Sometimes a story can have a happy ending when everyone is asking for something darker. Sometimes a dance reel can simply be a dance reel, without becoming an attempt to outdo everyone else's version.

The internet has given us an extraordinary number of ways to create and share. It would be a shame if we all ended up creating the same things.
