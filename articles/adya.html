Have you ever said something to your friend about buying a pair of shoes and then continued seeing shoes on your Instagram feed? Have you discussed a vacation spot and then started watching videos about these places on YouTube? There are moments that make us wonder if our phone is listening to us all the time or if social media knows us better than we know ourselves. The truth is, social media has indeed become an all-encompassing and all-powerful entity in the 21st century.

Instagram, Facebook, YouTube, Snapchat, TikTok, and many other social media applications are replete with information about their users. The likes, comments, searches, shares, scrolls, and clicks generate mountains of data telling the platforms what one likes and does not like. Even the videos that one does not watch or the articles one reads without clicking also inform the algorithms of what one is interested in and influence the content that starts showing up. Thus, it becomes increasingly apparent that social media is a spying entity, reading, listening, and watching everything one does online.

01 / THE MICROPHONE QUESTION

It is not that the phone in your pocket is listening to your conversations and feeding them to the algorithms. There may be a possibility that the applications can use your permission to access the microphone on your phone. However, the probability that every social media platform you use has access to your phone’s microphone and reads your chats or listens to your phone calls is highly unlikely. What really happens is that the algorithms guess what one wants to watch, read, or listen to by using the information one digitizes every day, such as location, browser history, cookies, and other data collected from third-party links and websites.

"The algorithms guess what one wants to watch, read, or listen to."
02 / THE RECOMMENDATION MACHINE

When one searches for a product, say, a dress on an e-commerce website, the algorithms of social media platforms use the information to advise similar dresses based on what one searched. One can also see many ads that show what one searched on different websites. For example, Facebook and Instagram use the information about the items one searches for or the articles they read to recommend similar content or products. The algorithms power these recommendations, which make the platforms aware of what users are interested in. While one might think these platforms eavesdrop on their conversations, they do not need to. The data generated when one browses online, the location, the photos uploaded, and the friends or family one interacts with all contribute to generating information that the algorithms use to make guesses about the users’ preferences.

03 / PREDICTING YOU

There is a more frightening element to how social media knows us; it can predict who we are. The simple act of following a page or posting a comment is enough for the applications to guess what one is interested in based on the pages one follows. Therefore, it can predict what one would want to watch or read and suggest it to the user. The algorithms use one’s every move on the applications to influence the content that starts showing up. It is also essential to note that the longer one uses the application, the more data it collects, the better it gets at predicting what one may be interested in, and the more ads it can show to the user.

04 / THE PRIVACY PROBLEM

There are privacy concerns associated with the extent of information that social media platforms collect about their users. Most applications ask the users to agree to their terms and conditions, which most people do without understanding what they are saying. Some users do not care about their privacy and post photographs that expose their location or sensitive personal information. However, data collection by social media platforms is an issue of concern since it affects everyone, irrespective of whether they value their privacy or not. It is important to note that the personal data of these users is not always safe. Some platforms may use this information for financial or malicious activities, such as selling it to third parties, cyber-espionage, cyber-surveillance, and data mining. The applications’ creators argue that collecting and selling data is necessary to fund the operations since their platforms do not charge the users for using their services. While their argument maybe valid, every individual must have a choice in giving away their data. It is also vital that the government keeps these entities in check.

"Every individual must have a choice in giving away their data."
05 / TAKING BACK CONTROL

Users can keep social media platforms in check in several ways. One can visit the settings of the applications and revoke the permissions given to the platforms. For example, one can disable access to the phone’s microphone or location. One can also limit ads or content that the applications recommend. Another way is ensuring that one does not give out sensitive information through the applications. Checking the accounts regularly and removing applications that one does not use is also a good way to protect personal data.

06 / THE ACTUAL PROBLEM

The biggest problem with the way phone listens to us is not that we are being overheard. However, it is that much of our personal data is available online, and it is too easy for social media platforms to access it. One does not have to ask permission to listen to someone, and therefore, social media platforms do not have to listen to what we say to know what we are interested in. Everything one does online generates data that feeds the algorithms of social media platforms, and therefore, one should be wary of what one puts online. The issue is not whether technology listens to us, but rather, how much of our data is out in the public domain, where it can be accessed by third parties.
